**Day 1: The Great Escape - From Office Fires to Chinese Whiskey**

When your week in IT feels like juggling flaming chainsaws, a Friday escape isn’t optional, it’s a survival instinct. By 5 PM sharp, I slammed the laptop shut and sprinted out like a fugitive escaping corporate captivity. The border crossing was smooth with long queues, the high-speed train to Xiamen was even smoother, and my energy level was somewhere between “zombie” and “please let me lie down.”	

Dinner dreams of wandering neon-lit streets in Xiamen for local snacks dissolved instantly. Reality served me a prepacked bowl of beef brisket rice from the train pantry, which honestly tasted like resignation with a hint of soy sauce. A tired body and fatigued mind can hardly sleep in the blink of an eye. But the night redeemed itself when I grabbed a tiny bottle of Chinese whiskey and, bless China, a ready-to-go cup of ice from the convenience store downstairs. I curled up in the hotel, sipped my drink, and rewatched Andaz Apna Apna. Amar-Prem therapy works every single time.


**Day 2: Mountains, Mud & Mulan - Transit Chaos, 700+ Year Old Architecture and a Superwoman**

The day began with an early-morning train from Xiamen to Nanjing, and I was feeling smug about my neatly crafted itinerary, courtesy of Gemini. It looked perfect on paper. But China’s ground reality? A whole different universe. The moment I stepped out of a no-escalator, stairs-only Nanjing Station (fun fact: the station is literally shaped like a Tulou), the plan collapsed like a badly built Jenga tower. No buses. No shared vans. No taxi stand. No signage. Just me, my backpack, and a growing suspicion that Gemini had never actually been to Fujian.

Enter DeepSeek, the real MVP of the day. While I stood there blinking at the empty station like a confused NPC (only seven people disembarked there), DeepSeek rebuilt my entire itinerary on the spot, routes, timings, alternatives, everything. Within minutes, I had a workable plan and a local tout lined up to drive me into the valleys. Crisis averted. Ego bruised. Adventure resumed. FYI, Didi has forsaken Nanjing.
My dream of renting an e-scooter and zooming through the mountains evaporated quickly; the region runs strictly on official shuttle buses and there is no concept of time in the Tulou Scenic Area. But once I surrendered to the system, the day unfolded beautifully. The villages were magical, and the people, mostly 70+, were the sweetest humans you’ll ever meet.

Tianluokeng greeted me with its iconic “Four Dishes and One Soup” layout, and I hiked down into the courtyards to feel the scale from the inside. It was amazing to see real people still living in the Tulous. It is not just a tourist attraction, but a livelihood for the people there. Yuchanglou, the 700-year-old leaning marvel, was a photographer’s playground, and the highlight of the day was an unforgettable lunch of Fried River Shrimp and Stuffed Tofu cooked by an 85-plus-year-old superwoman who runs her tiny home eatery entirely by herself. Taxia Village was pure serenity, a 4-5 km riverside walk with zero crowds and a soundtrack provided by the flowing stream.

![Xiamen-01](/images/Xiamen-01.png)

Right on cue, Fujian’s dramatic summer thunderstorms rolled in. Visibility dropped to zero, and the mountains turned into a watercolour blur. I skipped Hekeng, the actual shooting location of Mulan, because once you’re drenched, all earthen castles look identical anyway. I preponed my return ticket and rolled back into Xiamen while the evening was still young. Back in Xiamen, I did the only sensible thing after a day of ancient Tulous and thunderstorms: freshened up until I felt human again.

Later, I headed to Zhongshan Road Pedestrian Street. Nice, lively, and exactly like every other famous pedestrian street in China. The standout attraction? A 100 cm pork skewer roughly the distance from my stomach to the ground. Eating it felt less like dinner and more like a public performance.

I strolled along the waterfront with Gulangyu glowing across the water while the city lights put on a futuristic laser show. Beautiful.
My legs, however, were not impressed. After 27,000 steps, they were actively considering resignation. So, I consulted DeepSeek again. This time it sent me to a Thai massage place, and for once AI exceeded expectations. Soft music, chic interiors, and an absolutely magical massage. I walked in as an overworked project manager and walked out as a functioning member of society.
Grabbed some dumplings on the way back, poured a whiskey over convenience-store ice, and continued my nightly therapy session with Andaz Apna Apna.


**Day 3: Cannons, Scooters & A Train Betrayal**

The final day began lazily and with immense disappointment. The hotel breakfast featured only DIY noodles and almost nothing else. No eggs. No buns. No coffee. As a caffeine-dependent lifeform, I immediately abandoned the mission and found salvation in a Luckin Coffee and a street-side pork bun.
After checkout, I visited Hulishan Fortress, home to over 200 cannons overlooking the sea. The undisputed superstar is the giant Krupp cannon that once defended the coastline against Japanese attacks. For a while, I was happily transported back to another era.

Then came the trip highlight. Outside the fort, an auntie rented me an e-scooter. My dream of riding an e-scooter in Nanjing may have died, but Xiamen happily brought it back to life. For the next 20 km, I cruised up and down the coastline with beaches on one side and city views on the other. No schedule, no meetings, no Teams notifications. Just pure happiness. 

_Dreams stalled in Nanjing, my scooter plans turned to dust,_

_Then Xiamen winked, sent an auntie my way,_

_But for one glorious hour, I was Vin Diesel with a tourist pass and a weak heart._
_The sea hummed a soundtrack, the sunset put on a show,_
_And every kilometer whispered, “Mate, you really shouldn't go.”_
_Still, one thought kept following me through every gust and every bend,_
_I missed Sanjukta, my favourite travel partner, critic, and best friend._

After collecting my luggage and squeezing in a final foot spa near the station, I boarded my train home. Meanwhile, Typhoon Nalo was busy causing trouble across Hong Kong and moving inland into China. The train left on time. And then it happened. Somewhere between Xiamen and Shenzhen, the train abruptly stopped.

Not at a station. Not near a town. Just... nowhere. For over two and a half hours.
So much for my unwavering faith in the legendary punctuality of Chinese trains.
Eventually, we started moving again and rolled into Shenzhen more than two hours late.
Unfortunately, my connecting train to Hong Kong had already departed.

What followed was a classic travel finale: taxi to Shenzhen Bay, gliding through immigration, another taxi home, and a steadily increasing awareness that this little weather-related adventure was becoming expensive. To add insult to injury, they refused to refund the missed train because I was inside the two-hour policy window even through the delay was an "Act of God". My wallet and I respectfully disagreed with that policy. Maybe I should ask for settlement from God himself like Paresh Rawal. 

Thankfully, friends had already saved the day by sending food over. After three days of noodles, buns, dumplings, shrimp, tofu, train meals and various mystery snacks, the sight of biryani and chicken felt like being reunited with lost family members. After finally reached home, took a shower, demolished the food, and collapsed into bed.

The mountains, coastlines, ancient Tulous, scooters, massages, and accidental adventures would soon be replaced by Teams calls, inboxes, delivery timelines, and whatever fresh chaos awaited in the office. But that's a problem for future Souvik. And future Souvik can deal with it when he wakes up.
