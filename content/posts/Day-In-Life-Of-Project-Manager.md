The day actually is supposed to begin at 5:30 AM in a 24/7 gym. The target is to maintain a strict early-morning conditioning habit, mostly because pushing heavy metal plates around is the only way to prepare for the heavy lifting of carrying cross-functional teams across a project finish line. But reality is most days I can't even motivate myself to get out of bed to hit the gym. 

By the time I’m on the bus, commuting from home over to the office, I’m mentally mapping out the day. Once I reach office, there is a brief, magical window of time every morning right after that first black coffee hits my bloodstream. The sun is shining, my Wi-Fi is holding up, and I look at my meticulously crafted daily agenda with the naive optimism of a man who hasn’t yet opened Microsoft Teams.

I am a Senior Technical Project Manager. With 19+ years in the game, my job is essentially to herd bunch of highly capable individuals who prefer to work in silos through a maze of enterprise IT infrastructure as a team, all while pretending everything is perfectly fine.

Here is exactly how that usually goes.

<span style="font-size: 1.5rem; font-weight: bold;">The Morning: Coffee, Optimism and Delusion</span>

**8:45 AM: The Brief Delusion**
I start the day by reviewing my priorities. I feel organized. I feel optimistic. I have a color-coded matrix. This feeling will age terribly within the next forty-five minutes.

**9:30 AM: The Hydra Meeting**
First sync of the day. The goal was to align on two deliverables. We somehow leave with seven new action items, three existential questions about our architecture, and two additional spin-off meetings. I am essentially a meeting-generator NPC at this point.

**10:30 AM: The Art of the Polite Nudge**
I send a message to follow up on a critical task that was supposed to be done yesterday. I type "Just bringing this to the top of your inbox!" which is corporate speak for "Please do the thing we agreed you would do so I don't have to color a cell red in my status report and explain it to the steering committee."

**11:00 AM: The "Quick Question"**
A developer pings me: "Hey, quick question..."
Let me save you two decades of IT experience: There is no such thing as a quick question. A "quick question" is a trap door disguised as a greeting. It usually ends with redesigning a workflow automation logic branch or dousing a fire because someone missed adding a risk to the RAID log and now it has become an issue.

<span style="font-size: 1.5rem; font-weight: bold;">The Afternoon: Lunch is a Myth and Scope is a Suggestion</span>

**12:00 PM: The Great Shuffle**
I reprioritize the morning's priorities because the priorities have changed. The color-coded matrix from 8:45 AM is now completely obsolete.

**1:00 PM: Lunch? Adorable.**
A 10-minute quick lunch at my desk (sad, I know) and then substituting a 15-minute walk with another black coffee and pure adrenaline because time is of the essence and everyone needs everything yesterday.

**2:00 PM: The "Just One Little Thing" Incident**
A stakeholder casually asks if we can add "just one little button" to the UI. I take a deep breath and gently explain that adding just one little thing to the scope is, in fact, expanding the scope. The button requires a backend API change, a security review, and a blood sacrifice to the Agile gods. We negotiate it down to "Add to Backlog" for Phase 2 which might never get prioritized because by then it would have become redundant.

**3:00 PM: The Meta-Work**
I update the project plan to reflect the things that happened while I was busy updating the project plan. I am caught in a project management inception loop. The spreadsheets demand to be fed. I feel like Bill Murray in "Groundhog Day". 

<span style="font-size: 1.5rem; font-weight: bold;">The Evening: Retreat and Regret</span>

**4:00 PM: The Consensus Protocol**
I send out a written recap of everything we discussed in the 9:30 AM meeting, so everyone can agree on what we already agreed we just discussed. In corporate IT, memory is fleeting, but a paper trail is forever. Documentation is just defense.

**5:00 PM: The Seagull Stakeholder Strikes**
Right on schedule, a Senior Stakeholder swoops into my chat. This is the classic "Seagull Stakeholder" — they fly in out of nowhere, make a lot of noise about "shifting paradigms," drop a massive conceptual mess on my desk, and fly away. Today, they want a "high-level summary" of the rollout. I take a slow sip of my lukewarm coffee and gently point them toward the automated JIRA board I spent three weeks architecting specifically for this or the myriads of Status Update OneNote links that I have prepared meticulously for different level of stakeholders. They reply with a thumbs-up emoji. I check the analytics. They did not click the link.

**6:15 PM: The Final Mistake**
I close the laptop feeling oddly accomplished. I survived the day. The projects are actually moving forward, despite the chaos. I pack up and head for the Bus Stop. But right before I scan my Octopus card, I make the fatal error of opening Teams / Outlook on my phone one last time. A little red dot is waiting for me. Immediate, soul-crushing regret.

I swipe the notification away and put the phone in my pocket. Tomorrow, we do it all again. 🙃
You’d think after years of managing enterprise IT migrations, the most grueling part of my day would be a steering committee meeting. You would be wrong. The real test begins during the arduous bus journey home from the office, as I mentally brace myself for the hardest shift of the day.

When I tap my Octopus card and step off that bus back home, I am not clocking out. I am context-switching.

<span style="font-size: 1.5rem; font-weight: bold;">The 8 PM Sprint: From Corporate IT to Household Scrum Master</span>

**8:00 PM: The 90-Minute Sprint**
I walk through the door at 8:00 PM. Forget the nine-to-five; this next hour and a half is where the actual project management happens.

My development team is highly unpredictable. We have a 10-year-old suffering from chronic homework scope creep, and a 3-year-old acting as a human Chaos Monkey, randomly testing the household's patience infrastructure. There are no Agile frameworks for a toddler who suddenly decides his dinner is structurally unsound. The Product Owner (my wife) and I tag-team the chaos, putting out literal and figurative spills. We are strictly in survival mode.

**9:30 PM: The Forced Shutdown**
By 9:30 PM, we initiate a forced system shutdown. The kids are sent to bed—often against their will—a process that requires the kind of strategic negotiation skills usually reserved for vendor contracts.

This phase comes with a massive critical risk: Sanjukta goes in to get them to sleep. Will she emerge victorious and return to the living room, or will she accidentally fall asleep alongside them? It’s a 50/50 probability, and the outcome dictates the entire trajectory of my night.

**10:00 PM: The Personal Space or The Sandbox**
If the Product Owner survives the bedtime deployment and stays awake, 10:30 PM becomes our Retrospective. We sit down in the quiet apartment and map out our upcoming milestones. We plan out home parties, coordinate logistics for the next kids' party, and manage the backlog of gifts we need to buy. It’s high-level roadmap planning, just without the Microsoft Teams link and the color-coded spreadsheets.
But on those quiet nights when my Product Owner accidentally sleeps off with the kids? That is my sandbox time.
The house is completely silent. I crack open the laptop—not to update a status matrix, but to tinker with Common Man Chronicles on GitHub, figure out a CSS bug, or just stream a video in absolute, uninterrupted peace.

**12:00 Midnight: The Philosophical Retorpective**
Because ultimately, whether I’m migrating legacy enterprise systems for a shipping firm or negotiating a bedtime treaty with a toddler, the core job description is exactly the same. You manage the chaos, you mitigate the risks, and you try to keep your stakeholders from entirely losing their minds. My years of experience didn't explicitly cover how to handle a three-year-old's unhandled exception at the dinner table, but the underlying Agile principles surprisingly hold up. Enterprise deployments or living room survival—it's all just one continuous, unpredictable sprint. And sitting there in the quiet, looking at my real-life development team, I realize this is the only project I never want to close out.
