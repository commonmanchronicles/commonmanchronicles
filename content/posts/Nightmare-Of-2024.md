---
title: "A Nightmare Called '2024'"
date: 2025-07-23
---

Ah, another new year— that magical time when we all make resolutions, we know we’ll abandon by week two. I had the classic goals lined up: save money, cut expenses, and get fit. Spoiler alert: by the end of January, I was pretty much sure to remain the same couch potato I’ve always been. But 2024 had other plans for me.

Picture this: November 2023, my company decides to throw me a surprise party—by moving my job to India. So, I kick off 2024 jobless. My wife, on the other hand, was taking a sabbatical after our second child. I thought, “Hey, maybe this is the universe’s way of saying, ‘Congrats! Enjoy fatherhood!’” I mean, in Hong Kong, we don’t even get paternity leave, so I was ready to embrace my role as a full-time dad. For 10 years, I had been whining to my wife about needing a break. Finally, the universe granted me a month off! What could possibly go wrong? I’ve been in the industry for over 16 years—getting a new job would be as easy as pie, right? And AI hadn’t taken over my role as an IT Project Manager yet, so I was feeling pretty cool about my prospects.

My in-laws were staying with us after the baby’s arrival for moral support for my wife. The only real challenge? Keeping them blissfully unaware of my unemployment status. Easy peasy! I simply left every day during “office hours” to hang out at the library, boost my skills, and apply for jobs. Thanks to the work-from-home trend, I could squeeze in family time while pretending to be a diligent professional.

A month flew by, and I had applied to nearly 200 jobs. Zero replies. February rolled in, and I was still blissfully unconcerned. We had a family vacation planned for Chinese New Year in Da Nang, and I confidently assured my wife, “Of course we can afford it!”—a classic case of wishful thinking. The trip was a blast! It was our newborn’s first adventure and a fun time for our daughter, who was about to turn 8. We returned with our family bonds fortified and my optimism renewed. Surely, now that all the holidays were over, my resume would be the belle of the ball!

But, surprise! The status quo remained unchanged. I was up to 350 applications, and still no calls. I was flabbergasted. I even enlisted the help of AI to polish my resume and threw down some cash for LinkedIn Premium. Because nothing says “hire me” like a fancy profile, right? Determined to broaden my horizons, I started applying internationally. French companies? No problem! I just Google Translated my resume and cover letter into French. German and Spanish? Easy! And for Australia, the UK, and the USA, English was still in vogue, so I was good to go.

March rolled around, bringing with it my daughter’s birthday. We decided to keep it low-key because, let’s face it, we were about to dive headfirst into our savings. Thank goodness my wife had started working as a native English teacher a few years back; otherwise, we’d be living off instant noodles and dreams.

My close friends were in my corner, valiantly trying to boost my morale and connect me with opportunities. But then reality hit me like a 10,000-volt shock. Here I was, the guy who had always told his cousins, “Getting a job is a piece of cake!” Now I was the one sitting on the couch, jobless and living off my wife’s hard-earned savings. Irony, thy name is me.

My father had a bypass surgery a few years ago, so I didn’t want to dump my jobless woes on him. I mean, I may not be a mama's boy, but I used to share everything with my parents. Now, every time my dad asked how my job was going, I had to pull off my best poker face and lie, “Oh, it’s going great!” Talk about eating away at my sanity. By the end of March, I had applied to over 600 jobs. And guess what? I hadn’t heard back from 99% of them. Great odds, huh?

April came, bringing Easter holidays—a time when job applications take a back seat. After the festivities, I finally got some calls. But here’s the kicker: companies were offering me 40-50% of my former salary. I thought, “Surely, something is better than nothing!” But my wife, my pillar of strength through this chaos, advised me not to settle. If I accepted such lowball offers, there’d be no going back up the ladder.

After chatting with recruiters, I learned the hard truth: my profile wasn’t getting picked because I didn’t know the local language. And with jobs being outsourced to cheaper markets like China, the Philippines, and India, why would anyone hire me in Hong Kong? It made sense, but that realization sent me spiraling into panic attacks. I hid it from my wife because I knew she’d just worry, and honestly, I had no idea what she could do about it anyway.

By mid-May, our savings were dwindling. Everyone suggested cutting expenses by moving our daughter from private to public school. But I had seen my parents struggle to give me the best education on a tight budget, and I swore I wouldn’t compromise on my daughter's schooling. So, there I was, begging for jobs like a contestant on a game show with no prizes. Then came a session with a well-known recruiter who suggested I work in a restaurant if I wanted to stay in Hong Kong. My self-respect and ego were crushed into tiny pieces—like a soda can that’s been run over by a truck. That was the day I broke down.

June arrived, and I decided to send my family back to India while I stayed behind to keep searching for work. At least that would cut down on family expenses. My wife suggested we all move back and start fresh, but I wasn’t ready to throw in the towel. I wanted to prove I could land a job, so we postponed our trip until the end of June. I promised that if I was still jobless by then, we would return to India. By this time, I had applied to over 1,000 job opportunities and heard back from a handful. I had multiple interviews only to learn at the last minute, “Oh sorry, no vacancy.” And I still hadn’t told my parents about my situation. July loomed closer, and we had to plan our son’s rice ceremony—a big, expensive event in India. So here I was, stuck in a whirlwind of job applications, family expectations, and the ever-looming pressure of our son’s big celebration. What could possibly go wrong next?

But lo and behold, the sun finally decided to shine! By the end of June, I stumbled upon a position for an IT Project Manager at a shipping company. Never thought I’d venture into the shipping world, but desperate times call for desperate measures, right? I got the call for an interview, and after prepping like I was heading into battle (six months of practice will do that), I was ready to dazzle them. The interview was with the CTO and CFO, and let me tell you, I must have pulled out all the stops because I left them impressed. The job was mine! Sure, the pay was a tad lower than my previous gig, but who cares? I was finally employed! 

Everything got finalized in just seven days, and off we went to India in July for the rice ceremony, job offer in hand. When we reached home, I decided it was time to spill the beans about the rollercoaster ride my wife and I had been on for the past six months. My parents were shocked we hadn’t lost our minds or made any regrettable life choices along the way. We celebrated the rice ceremony in style, using up every last penny we had left. It was a grand affair—because why not go out with a bang after all that drama?

I traveled back to Hong Kong solo this time. My daughter’s summer break had started, and my wife needed some quality time with her parents. I joined the new company on my birthday—talk about a double celebration! We had endured enough pain to finally see that light at the end of the tunnel, and it was glorious. Victory was ours!

People often assume that life in a foreign land is easy. They think, “Oh, you’re earning in foreign currency; you must be the richest and happiest!” But little do they understand the daily struggles we face. In those moments, I found immense gratitude for my wife, who remained my constant anchor. She experienced her own moments of helplessness, doubt, and panic, yet she remained steadfast. For that, I will forever be grateful to her. I wanted to express to her what Tom Cruise famously said in Jerry Maguire: “I love you. You complete me.”

The next five months passed swiftly. My family was back in Hong Kong, and we slowly regained our footing. Life seemed to be looking up. My child didn’t have to move to a public school, and for a while, the sun shone brightly. We thought the worst was behind us when calamity struck once more.

This time, it wasn’t my job that was at stake; it was something far more precious. I still remember the day—December 5, 2024. I went to the office as usual when I received a call from a neighbor in India, where my parents were staying. My father had suffered a sudden heart failure and had collapsed. They rushed him to the emergency room, but his heart had stopped on the way. Though the doctors managed to resuscitate him, he had to be put on a ventilator.

In that moment, I felt the earth shift beneath my feet. I called my wife and told her to pack our bags; we needed to return immediately. I was unsure how my boss would react to my sudden trip to India, especially since I had only been at the job for four to five months. But in that moment, nothing else mattered. To my relief, my boss turned out to be the most compassionate and understanding person I had ever worked for.

Getting tickets that day proved nearly impossible. To add to the chaos, my bank canceled my credit card due to a fraud alert triggered by my attempts to book tickets from a less popular website. Finally, using my Indian credit card, I managed to secure tickets, and we arrived in India that very night. A doctor friend arranged for us to see my father in the middle of the night, outside visiting hours. There he lay on the ventilator, unaware of my presence.

The next four days were a relentless nightmare as his organs began to fail one by one. My daughter, her grandfather’s pet, asked whether I had told him that “Sonai has come to see him. He needs to come back.” What could I say to an innocent eight-year-old? That her grandfather might never wake up? Yet, a part of me clung to hope. We hoped for a miracle, believing that the doctors could perform some form of magic. After all, my father embodied goodness; he had never fought with anyone, never harmed a soul, always helping those in need. Surely, bad things don’t happen to good people.

On December 10, 2024, the doctors called my mother and me into a room. They informed us that my father was brain dead and would never recover. No miracle, no medicine, no magic could revive him. They asked me to sign a consent form to remove him from the ventilator, allowing him to pass away peacefully. The person who had been my rock my entire life was now lying still, like a stone. I sincerely hope no one ever has to endure the pain of a son signing the last testament for his father. That night, he passed away. The doctor assured me it was a peaceful demise, but I remained in denial. He was 69, approaching 70. He loved me, he loved us—how could he leave so suddenly? In my grief, denial turned to anger. He had left my mother and me, escaping this world without warning. My wife tried to reason with me, suggesting he wouldn’t have wanted it this way, but I was shattered.

Never in my life had I been to a crematorium. I had never witnessed anyone’s last rites. The first time I experienced this was for my own father. The following day was a trance. I found myself bringing his cold body from the morgue to our home, surrounded by relatives crying their hearts out as we took him to the cremation ghat. I felt like a zombie, merely listening to the processes, the rules, the regulations, unable to cry. I was a man. Men shouldn’t cry. My father had taught me to be strong, to take care of everyone.

The rituals continued for 13 days. My father was an atheist at heart, yet whenever there was a death in the family, he would adhere to all the rules and rituals with a sense of duty. I, too, am an atheist—the apple doesn’t fall far from the tree. But I made the decision to follow all the customs during this time. There would be no shortcuts; my father had never taught me to take the easy way out.

Meanwhile, my mother was a wreck. She was utterly inconsolable, having never been alone her entire life. Before her marriage, she had lived with her parents and sisters. For 40 years of marriage, she had relied entirely on my father. Despite his constant encouragement for her to be independent, she never learned to use the ATM or manage finances on her own. In light of this, I knew what I had to do: I booked her a dependent visa and planned to bring her to Hong Kong to stay with us.

After 15 days, I needed to return to work, so I traveled back to Hong Kong with my daughter. I knew she would be devastated to see her grandmother in such a state, and if she stayed in India, she would miss her grandfather even more. My wife remained in India to care for my mother until the visa arrived and the documentation process for her pension and bank account management was completed. My wife was like June Carter Cash, guiding me through one of the toughest periods of my life. She was now my rock, steady and supportive, fighting for our family in quiet but powerful ways. She embodies what Mac in Juno so eloquently stated: "the best thing you can do is find a person who loves you for exactly what you are. Good mood, bad mood, ugly, pretty... the right person will still think the sun shines out your ass". She makes me utter the same words that Jack Nicholson said in As Good As It Gets – “You make me want to be a better man”. Once again, she proved to be the best partner I could ask for, often showing more strength than I felt I had.


The year 2024 began with me jobless and ended with me fatherless. It was a year I wish I could erase from my memory—a year I wouldn’t wish upon my worst enemy, not ever.
